Why Advice-Only Financial Planning Might Disrupt Traditional Wealth Management

Same Advice. Same Investments. Same Custodian. Different Fees.

What if you could keep your investments exactly as they are, receive professional financial advice, and avoid paying thousands of dollars annually for investment management you may not need?

You can. It's called Advice-Only financial planning.

First, What Is Advice-Only?

While there is no formal regulatory definition of Advice-Only, for purposes of this article, an Advice-Only advisor is defined as an advisor who exclusively provides financial advice without managing client investment portfolios, exercising trading authority, taking custody of client assets, or receiving commissions from selling financial products.

Three commonly used terms are worth distinguishing:

  • Fee-Based: Advisors may receive advisory fees and commissions from selling financial products.
  • Fee-Only: Advisors receive advisory fees rather than product-sales commissions. Many also manage investment portfolios, charging a percentage of assets under management (AUM), flat fees, or other fees.
  • Advice-Only: Advisors provide financial advice without managing client portfolios or receiving product-sales commissions. Compensation generally comes from hourly, flat, project-based, or retainer fees.

Advice-Only is a narrower form of Fee-Only financial planning. Not all Fee-Only advisors are Advice-Only.

Some firms offer hourly or project-based financial planning alongside traditional investment management. Under the definition used here, those firms offer planning-only engagements, but they aren't exclusively Advice-Only firms.

It's also important to distinguish investment management from how that service is priced. Advisors who manage portfolios may charge a percentage of AUM or a flat fee that doesn't automatically increase as the portfolio grows.

If you prefer to delegate investment management, consider comparing flat-fee arrangements with percentage-based AUM fees. You may receive similar services without paying more simply because your portfolio is larger. The scope of services, total costs, and fee adjustments still matter.

Why Now?

Financial advice, investment management, and custody are separate functions that have historically been bundled together. Advances in technology, low-cost investments, and commission-free brokerage trading have made it easier to separate them. Consumers no longer need to pay for the entire bundle when they only want one part of it.

Is Advice-Only Just for People With Smaller Portfolios?

There's a misconception that Advice-Only financial planning is primarily for people with smaller portfolios who can't afford traditional investment management.

Nothing could be further from the truth.

While Advice-Only financial planning makes professional advice more accessible to people with smaller portfolios, the model also resembles how affluent individuals routinely pay for other professional services.

When you hire a CPA for tax planning or an attorney for estate planning, you generally pay an hourly, flat, or negotiated fee. You don't have to transfer your investment accounts to them, and their compensation isn't automatically tied to your net worth.

Why should financial planning be any different?

Someone with $5 million or $10 million in investments may have more complex financial planning needs than someone with $500,000. That doesn't necessarily mean they need someone else managing their portfolio, or that the cost of financial advice should increase proportionately with their assets.

Advice-Only planning isn't about how much money you have. It's about separating the value of professional advice from the cost of managing investments.

A $1 Million Comparison: Bundled Management vs. Advice-Only

Consider two investors, each with $1 million invested in the same portfolio. The investments are identical, and the custodian might even be the same.

  • The Bundled 1% AUM Approach: The first investor hires a traditional financial advisor to manage the portfolio, paying an annual fee of 1% of assets under management. That costs $10,000 annually, or $100,000 over ten years, assuming the portfolio value remains constant, without accounting for the potential investment growth lost on those fees.
  • The Self-Directed + Advice-Only Approach: The second investor manages the investments independently, periodically rebalancing and making adjustments as needed, while hiring an Advice-Only planner for targeted guidance. Advice-Only planners may charge hourly fees, often ranging from a few hundred dollars per hour, or fixed fees for specific projects. The total cost depends on the scope and frequency of the advice received.

The difference? The first investor pays $100,000 in AUM fees over ten years, while the second pays only for the planning services they choose to use. Depending on their needs, those costs could be substantially lower.

The traditional advisor may provide additional services, including ongoing portfolio monitoring, investment implementation, and financial planning. Those services can have real value.

You don't necessarily need an ongoing investment management relationship.

You can hire professional guidance when you need help with investments, retirement planning, taxes, insurance, estate planning, or other financial decisions.

Does Investing Really Need to Be So Complicated?

The financial services industry has a tendency to downplay simplicity and oversell complexity.

Vanguard founder Jack Bogle championed low-cost, broadly diversified index investing. A simple three-fund portfolio can provide exposure to thousands of stocks and bonds without complicated investment strategies or expensive ongoing management.

Bogle frequently emphasized the importance of patience and resisting the temptation to trade unnecessarily. His advice was simple:

"Don't do something. Just stand there!"

Source: John C. Bogle, Don't Do Something. Just Stand There (2004).

https://johncbogle.com/speeches/JCB_AIC0904.pdf

The point isn't that investors should never make changes. It's that a properly designed portfolio generally shouldn't require constant trading, even though periodic reviews remain important.

Think of it like your physical health. You don't necessarily need a doctor monitoring you every day, but periodic checkups can help identify problems, evaluate changes, and determine whether adjustments are appropriate.

Why not take a similar approach to your investments?

An Advice-Only advisor can periodically review your portfolio, evaluate your allocation, assess investment costs, and recommend changes when warranted or confirm that leaving everything exactly as it is makes sense. You can also obtain a second opinion from an Advice-Only advisor without committing to ongoing investment management fees.

Fee-Based and Fee-Only investment managers may also feel pressure to recommend changes or demonstrate ongoing value through activity, even when no portfolio changes are necessary.

For many investors, the cumulative cost of periodic advice may be substantially less than paying an annual percentage of their portfolio for continuous management.

Today, investors have access to self-directed investing through Vanguard, Fidelity, Charles Schwab, Morgan Stanley's E*TRADE, and M1, as well as platforms offered by many large banks, including JPMorgan Chase, Bank of America, Citibank, Wells Fargo, and U.S. Bank.

These platforms generally offer commission-free online trading for eligible stocks and ETFs, although other fees and restrictions may apply. Some also offer automated investing and rebalancing.

Millions of Americans already manage investments through employer-sponsored retirement plans without hiring a personal investment manager.

Not everyone has the time, interest, or temperament to manage investments independently. Some investors simply prefer to delegate that responsibility.

The decision to hire an investment manager isn't necessarily about portfolio complexity. A sophisticated portfolio can be self-directed, while a simple portfolio can be professionally managed. It's about whether you want or need someone else to perform that work.

The problem isn't complexity. It's selling complexity when simplicity would serve the client just as well.

A straightforward investment strategy isn't necessarily inferior. It may simply be less profitable for the financial services industry.

Follow the Money: Incentives Can Create Conflicts of Interest

The financial services industry loves to talk about aligning the advisor's interests with the client's.

With percentage-based AUM fees, advisors generally earn more as the value of managed assets increases. Flat-fee investment managers don't necessarily have that same incentive tied to portfolio value, although they may still benefit from attracting and retaining management clients.

Percentage-based AUM advisors also continue earning fees when your portfolio loses value, although the dollar amount of those fees generally decreases.

If you make money, the advisor makes money. If you lose money, the advisor still makes money. Is that really complete alignment of interests?

More importantly, both percentage-based and flat-fee investment management models create financial incentives to attract and retain clients and their assets.

Consider an advisor recommending that you transfer your investments into an advisor-managed account. Is that recommendation based entirely on your needs, or is it influenced by the recurring revenue the advisor would receive?

The recommendation might be entirely appropriate. The financial incentive still exists.

The same conflict can arise when considering whether to pay off a mortgage, purchase real estate, buy an annuity, roll an old 401(k) into an advisor-managed IRA, or make another financial decision that affects managed assets.

Here's a simple way to think about it:

If you're compensated for selling insurance, you have an incentive to sell insurance. If you're compensated for managing investments, you have an incentive to sell investment management.

Why should consumers assume that one compensation model is inherently more objective than the other?

Fee-Only advisors often emphasize that they don't earn commissions from selling financial products. That's an important distinction, but eliminating commissions doesn't eliminate conflicts of interest.

Replacing a commission with an ongoing AUM fee doesn't magically eliminate the incentive to sell. It changes what you're selling.

Once an advisor manages your portfolio, changing the relationship may involve transferring accounts, changing investments, or potentially triggering taxes. That friction can discourage clients from switching advisors. In the financial services industry, this is sometimes referred to as creating "stickiness."

None of this means investment managers are dishonest or that investment management lacks value. It means consumers should recognize the incentives behind the recommendations they receive.

Advice-Only: Separating Advice From Investment Management

Advice-Only financial planning removes the direct financial incentive to accumulate and retain managed assets.

You maintain control of your accounts, choose where your investments are held, and execute your own transactions.

An Advice-Only advisor can recommend specific investments, review your portfolio, develop an asset allocation strategy, and help implement changes.

For example, an advisor can use screen sharing to walk you through a transaction at Schwab, Fidelity, or another brokerage platform while you retain responsibility for placing the trade.

Advice-Only financial planning is a collaborative relationship. The advisor provides professional guidance, explains the available options, and helps you make informed decisions. You remain responsible for deciding what to do and whether to implement the recommendations.

Advice-Only advisors can also help with retirement readiness, Social Security, Medicare, tax planning, cash flow, debt management, insurance, estate planning, and other financial decisions.

They can provide independent second opinions on recommendations from existing investment managers, including whether the services and fees are appropriate.

Advice-Only isn't entirely free of conflicts. Hourly advisors may have incentives to spend more time, while flat-fee advisors may have incentives to limit the scope of work.

An Advice-Only advisor might also favor self-directed investing when a client would be better served by delegating portfolio management.

Every compensation model has conflicts and creates incentives. The objective is to understand those conflicts and incentives rather than pretend they don't exist.

Is Advice-Only Right for You?

To help determine whether a relationship with an Advice-Only advisor might be right for you, ask yourself the following questions:

Do you already manage your own investments, or do you want to learn how with professional guidance?

Do you want a second opinion on your current portfolio, tax strategy, or retirement readiness without moving your accounts?

Do you prefer paying a transparent fee for specific advice rather than an ongoing percentage of assets?

Do you value having a fiduciary professional to consult when making major financial decisions involving investments, real estate, equity compensation, Social Security, estate planning, and other financial matters?

If you answered yes to any of these questions, it might be worthwhile to have a conversation with an Advice-Only advisor.

Form ADV: A Practical Screening Tool

Registered investment advisory firms disclose their services, compensation, and business practices through Form ADV.

You can review these disclosures through the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) at its Investment Adviser Public Disclosure (IAPD) website:

https://adviserinfo.sec.gov/

Search for the advisory firm by name, open its latest Form ADV filing, and look for Part 1A, Item 5.E: Compensation Arrangements.

The firm must identify all applicable compensation arrangements. The choices are:

  • (1) A percentage of assets under management
  • (2) Hourly charges
  • (3) Subscription fees for a newsletter or periodical
  • (4) Fixed fees other than subscription fees
  • (5) Commissions
  • (6) Performance-based fees
  • (7) Other compensation

Here's a quick litmus test: If boxes (1), (5), or (6) are checked, the firm generally does not qualify as exclusively Advice-Only under the definition used in this article.

Notice that a firm charging flat fees for investment management may have box (4) checked without checking box (1). That's why the compensation checklist alone cannot establish whether a firm is Advice-Only.

A firm that passes this first test isn't necessarily Advice-Only. Review the remaining disclosures.

Additional Form ADV checks:

  • Part 1A, Item 5.F:Look for regulatory assets under management. An exclusively Advice-Only firm should generally report $0.
  • Part 1A, Item 5.G:Review the types of advisory services, including financial planning and portfolio management.
  • Part 1A, Item 9:Check whether the firm reports regulatory custody of client assets.
  • Part 2A, Items 4, 5, 10, and 15:Review the firm's services, compensation, affiliations, and custody practices.

Zero regulatory AUM isn't conclusive. A firm may offer investment management without currently managing any assets. Likewise, an advisor may manage investments without having regulatory custody.

Custody, investment management authority, and the location of client assets are different concepts.

The most useful question to ask is straightforward:

Does your firm manage client portfolios, have authority to execute trades, charge asset-based fees, or receive commissions from selling financial products?

Check FINRA Registration

Another useful screening step is determining whether the individual is currently registered with a broker-dealer.

The Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (FINRA) is a self-regulatory organization overseeing broker-dealers and their registered representatives.

If an advisor is currently registered as a broker-dealer representative, that person generally would not qualify as Advice-Only under the definition used here, even if they also offer financial planning for a separate fee.

Some financial professionals are dually registered as both investment adviser representatives and broker-dealer representatives.

You can check registrations and professional backgrounds through FINRA BrokerCheck:

https://brokercheck.finra.org/

Appearing in BrokerCheck doesn't automatically mean someone is currently associated with a broker-dealer. The database includes historical registrations. Check the individual's current registration status.

Make Sure the Advisor Is a Good Fit

Before hiring an advisor, confirm that the advisor is legally permitted to serve clients in your state, whether through SEC or state registration or an applicable exemption.

Also consider whether the advisor is accepting new clients, offers virtual or in-person meetings, and has convenient availability, including evenings or weekends if needed.

Review credentials, experience, pricing, minimum fees, and ongoing commitments.

An introductory meeting, preferably at no cost, can help determine whether the relationship is a good fit.

The Bottom Line: Pay for What You Actually Need

Investment management is a legitimate service. For some investors, it's worth paying for.

If you want someone to manage your investments, compare both flat-fee and percentage-based arrangements. If you prefer to manage them yourself, consider whether Advice-Only planning can provide the professional guidance you need.

Professional financial advice doesn't require handing over management of your investments.

You can maintain your accounts at your preferred financial institution, manage your own investments, and hire a qualified professional to help with the financial decisions that matter.

The financial services industry has built a substantial business around convincing consumers that professional advice and investment management belong together. They don't have to.

Before paying someone thousands of dollars annually to manage your investments, ask whether you're paying for a service you genuinely need or simply accepting the industry's preferred business model.

Whether you have $100,000 or $10 million invested, you don't have to choose between managing your own investments and receiving professional financial advice.

With Advice-Only financial planning, you can do both.
